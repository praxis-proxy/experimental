The third slide separates the policies that are often incorrectly collapsed into one score.

Admission asks whether a provider can receive traffic. Grouping asks which providers are allowed to compete together. Local placement asks how traffic retained on the preferred tier is distributed. Burst policy asks how much traffic should leave that tier. Overflow policy asks where the burst traffic should go.

For example, Grid might decide that thirty percent of new traffic needs external capacity. A separate overflow policy can divide that thirty percent among several external providers using an independent ratio.

That means the amount of traffic that bursts and the destination of that traffic are separate controls. Changing the burst amount does not silently change the external ratio. Changing the external ratio does not change the condition that caused bursting.

The same separation applies to locality, queue pressure, health, and cost. A provider that is unhealthy cannot be restored by a large weight. A fallback provider does not compete with local providers when the preferred group still has new-request capacity.

Grid computes these policies into a versioned overlay. The request gateway does not recompute the policy. It selects locally from the accepted groups and weights.
